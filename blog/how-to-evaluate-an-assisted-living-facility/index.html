How to Evaluate an Assisted Living Facility

By Cory Fosco · April 25, 2026

How to evaluate an assisted living facility before you tour: six dimensions to assess, the questions to ask about staffing and cost, and what families miss.

A well-run assisted living facility and a poorly run one can look nearly identical on a tour. Both will have a clean lobby, a friendly admissions director, and attractive rooms. The differences that actually predict quality are harder to see, and almost impossible to evaluate if you do not know what to look for.

After thirty-four years working inside this system as a social worker, admissions director, and healthcare executive, I can tell you exactly what separates a facility that will take good care of your loved one from one that will not. I have worked the other side of the admissions table hundreds of times. I know what a tour is designed to show you, and I know what it leaves out.

This guide walks through six dimensions of evaluation, in the order that matters most. If you do this work before your first tour, you will ask better questions, notice things other families miss, and make a decision you can stand behind.

What research should you do before touring an assisted living facility?

Do not walk into any facility without doing research first. This is not about reading brochures. It is about looking at publicly available data that most families do not know exists.

For skilled nursing facilities, inspection reports are public. Go to Medicare's Care Compare tool at medicare.gov/care-compare and search by location. You will find star ratings, health inspection results, staffing data, and quality measures. These reports are not perfect, but they surface patterns. A facility with repeated citations for the same issue is telling you something. Pay particular attention to the severity and scope of deficiencies, not just the number of them.

If you are still deciding which level of care your loved one actually needs, the four types of senior care explains how assisted living differs from independent living, memory care, and skilled nursing. The free Four Signal Categories Checklist is a useful pre-tour tool for sorting which signals are present in the first place.

For assisted living communities, publicly available data is more limited because regulation happens at the state level and requirements vary enormously. Check your state's department of health or licensing division for any complaints or enforcement actions. Some states publish inspection results online. Some do not. If yours does not, call the state licensing agency and ask about the facility's compliance history. You have the right to this information.

Look at online reviews, but read them carefully. A single bad review may reflect one person's experience. A pattern of reviews describing the same problem, especially around staffing, responsiveness, or communication, is worth paying attention to.

Check whether the facility has changed ownership recently. Ownership transitions can disrupt staffing, policies, and culture. A facility that was excellent under one operator may not be the same under another. This is exactly what happened to one of the families I write about in The Question of When: a community she had researched and chosen had changed ownership by the time she needed it, and the reviews had turned.

What should you look for during an assisted living tour?

The admissions director's job is to present the community well. That is not dishonest. It is their role. Your job is to look past the presentation and observe what daily life actually feels like.

Watch how staff interact with residents. Are they using first names? Are they making eye contact? Do they seem rushed, or do they stop when a resident needs something? The relationship between aides and residents is the single best indicator of a facility's culture, and it is almost impossible to fake during a tour if you are paying attention.

Look at the residents themselves. Do they seem engaged, or are they sitting in front of a television with no interaction? Are people in common areas, or is the building quiet in a way that feels empty? Walk through the dining area during a meal if you can. Does the food look like something you would eat? Is the pace relaxed or hurried? Are staff assisting residents who need help, or are trays sitting untouched?

Notice the physical environment beyond the lobby. Walk the hallways. Look at the condition of resident rooms if you are shown one. Are handrails in place? Are hallways clear and well-lit? Does the building smell clean, or is there a persistent odor being masked by air freshener? These are small things, but they accumulate.

Ask to see the activity calendar. Look at how many activities are scheduled per day, whether they happen on weekends, and whether they involve leaving the building. A robust activity program is a sign that the community invests in quality of life, not just safety.

What questions should you ask about staffing in assisted living?

Staffing is the single biggest predictor of quality in any care setting. Ask these questions directly, and pay attention to how comfortably they are answered.

  • What is the staff-to-resident ratio during the day? At night? On weekends? The answers should be specific numbers, not vague reassurances. If someone says "we always have enough staff," that is not an answer.
  • What is the turnover rate for direct care staff? High turnover means your loved one will be cared for by a rotating cast of people who do not know them well. Consistency matters enormously, especially for someone whose cognition is changing.
  • How are staff trained? Is there ongoing education, or just an initial orientation? Ask specifically about dementia training, fall prevention, and de-escalation. These are the situations that test a staff's preparedness.
  • Who is on site at night? Is there a licensed nurse on the premises twenty-four hours, or is there a nurse on call? This distinction matters more than most families realize. On call means someone will come if needed. On site means someone is already there.
  • What happens when a resident falls? Ask for the specific protocol. Who responds? How quickly? How is the family notified? How is the incident documented? Falls are among the most common events in assisted living, and how a facility handles them tells you a great deal about how they handle everything.

What questions should you ask administration about costs and contracts?

Beyond staffing, there are operational questions that directly affect your loved one's experience and your family's financial exposure.

  • What is included in the base monthly rate, and what costs extra? Get the full fee schedule in writing. Common add-on charges include medication management, incontinence care, escort to meals, help with transfers, and laundry. A facility quoting $4,500 a month may actually cost $6,500 once services are added. This surprises families constantly. It is also worth understanding what Medicare actually covers for long-term care before you assume any of these costs will be reimbursed.
  • How often do rates increase, and by how much? Ask for the history. Some communities raise rates 3-5% annually. Some raise them when a resident's care level changes. Both are common, but you need to understand which model applies and what triggers an increase.
  • What are the terms of the residency agreement? This is a contract. Read it. What is the discharge policy? Under what circumstances can the facility ask a resident to leave? How much notice is required? What happens to the deposit? Most families sign the admissions agreement in a hurry, often in a hallway while under stress. Do not do this. Take it home. Read it. Ask questions.
  • What is the policy on outside care providers? Some assisted living communities allow families to bring in private duty home care aides to supplement the facility's care. Others do not. If your loved one's needs are on the higher end of what assisted living can manage, this flexibility may matter.
  • What is the process if care needs exceed what the community can provide? Assisted living communities have the right to ask a resident to leave if their needs go beyond what the facility is licensed to provide. This is one of the most consequential things families do not know until it happens. Ask how they handle this transition, how much notice is given, and whether they help coordinate the move. The signals that this line is coming are covered in when to move from assisted living to a nursing home.

What are the warning signs of a poorly run assisted living facility?

Not every warning sign means you should walk away. But each one deserves a direct question and an honest answer.

The facility discourages unscheduled visits. A community that wants you to call ahead every time is a community that wants to control what you see.

Staffing questions are deflected or answered vaguely. If they cannot give you a specific ratio or turnover number, they either do not track it or do not want you to know.

The admissions process feels rushed. Pressure to sign quickly, to put down a deposit today, to commit before you have seen other options. Good facilities do not need to pressure you. They have waitlists.

Residents appear unattended for extended periods. Especially during meals or in common areas.

The physical environment is inconsistent. The lobby is immaculate but the hallways or resident areas show wear, disrepair, or neglect.

Staff do not seem to know the residents. If aides cannot tell you something about the person they are caring for, the care is task-based, not relationship-based.

The facility cannot clearly explain what happens when needs change. This is a sign that transitions are handled reactively, not proactively.

Why should you visit an assisted living facility more than once?

I always tell families: even the best facilities have down days. One hour-long visit may not give you the full picture. If you can, visit once during the day and once in the early evening. The first visit answers your planned questions. The second shows you what the place feels like when things are quieter, when the admissions team may not be expecting you, and when you can see how evening routines actually work.

If an evening visit is not possible, try visiting on a weekend. Weekend staffing is often thinner than weekday staffing, and the experience your loved one has on a Saturday morning is just as important as the one they have on a Tuesday afternoon.

What to do after an assisted living tour

Write down your impressions immediately. What did you notice? What surprised you? What made you uncomfortable? What question did you forget to ask?

Then talk it through with your family. The decision usually gets clearer when you compare your own observations, not just what each community told you. If the conversation with your loved one about assisted living still feels far off, how to talk to a parent about assisted living is the starting point.

The goal is not to find a perfect place. There is no perfect place. The goal is to find the right fit for the person you love, at this stage of their needs, with staff you trust and a culture that treats your family member as a person, not a room number.
